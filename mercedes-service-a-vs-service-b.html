Mercedes Repair Guide

Mercedes Service A vs Service B

The dash says "Service B due". Here is what that means, what each service includes, why the letter matters less than the engine under the hood, and what we look at that the sheet leaves out.

By the technicians at German Performance · September 15, 2026

Every Mercedes-Benz built since the late 1990s carries a service computer, ASSYST and later ASSYST Plus, that counts down to the next service and names it with a letter. Owners bring the letter to us for Mercedes repair in Snellville and ask what it costs; we ask what engine it is, because the letter is only the outline. This guide explains how the system counts, what Service A and Service B actually cover, which oil and fluid approvals your particular engine and transmission were built for, and the inspection items we add because the cars that come through here have taught us to.

How ASSYST Plus decides what is due

ASSYST Plus keeps a countdown in miles and in days and shows whichever runs out first, with the letter of the service that is next. On the cars sold here the pattern is one service a year or every 10,000 miles, alternating A and B: A at the first year, B at the second, A at the third and so on. The counter is reset by the technician after the work, through the cluster or through XENTRY, and the reset records which service was performed; a car whose display shows an A when it should show a B has usually been reset by hand without the work behind it.

The system is honest about time, which matters more than owners expect. A Mercedes-Benz that covers 4,000 miles a year still needs the oil changed annually, because engine oil degrades with heat cycles and moisture, not only with distance. The countdown will reach zero on the calendar long before it reaches zero on the odometer, and it is right to.

What Service A includes

Service A is the smaller of the two. Engine oil and filter to the approved sheet; a check and correction of every fluid level; tire pressures and condition; a brake inspection of pads, rotors and lines; a check of the lights, wipers and washers; the service reminder reset. On most engines since the mid-2000s the oil filter is a paper cartridge in a housing on top of the engine rather than a spin-on canister, and the housing's O-rings are replaced with it. Most of these cars also have no dipstick; the level is read through the cluster once the engine is warm, and we verify it that way after the fill.

The oil is the part that is easy to get wrong. Mercedes-Benz approves oils by sheet number, and the sheet is printed in the booklet and on the cap of any oil that holds the approval. Most gasoline engines from the M272 and M273 era onward call for MB 229.5; the newer direct-injection M276 and M278, and the four-cylinder M274, are typically listed for 229.5 or the low-ash 229.51 and 229.52, which the catalysts and particulate filters on later cars depend on; the current M256 and M264 families moved to the thinner 229.71. The number is not a formality. Our German car oil change page explains what we fill with; on a Mercedes-Benz we look the engine code up in the operating-fluids list linked in the sources below, not the badge on the trunk.

What Service B adds

Service B is Service A plus the two-year items. Brake fluid is the important one: Mercedes-Benz specifies a DOT 4 Plus fluid changed every two years, because brake fluid absorbs water from the air whether the car is driven or not, and water in the fluid lowers its boiling point and corrodes the ABS and stability-control hydraulics from the inside. The cabin (combination) filter is replaced. The engine air filter is inspected and replaced by the booklet's interval. The inspection is longer: suspension and steering components, the exhaust, the underbody, the drive belt and its tensioner, the cooling system and the battery.

Some items are neither A nor B but sit on their own mileage in the booklet: spark plugs, the fuel filter on older cars, the transmission fluid on many. This is why the letter is only an outline. Two cars can both be due a Service B and one of them is also due plugs and a transmission service, which changes the day considerably. Mercedes-Benz USA publishes the maintenance booklet for every model year on the page in the sources; it is worth ten minutes with your own.

The transmission fluid is not lifetime

The 7G-TRONIC (the 722.9, fitted from 2003) and the 9G-TRONIC that followed were both described as sealed for life when new. The maintenance booklets have since put a mileage on the fluid and filter for most of these cars, and our experience is that the interval is the difference between a transmission that lasts and one that does not. The 722.9's electronic control unit, the conductor plate, sits in the fluid, and old fluid takes its speed sensors and the valve body with it. A service here is fluid to the approved sheet, the filter, the pan gasket and, on the 722.9, the level set by temperature with XENTRY, because the correct level is only correct at one fluid temperature. The transmission repair page covers what we do when it has been left too long.

The older 722.6 five-speed has one more item of its own: the electrical connector on the side of the case has an O-ring that hardens and lets fluid wick up the harness into the transmission control module. We check it at every service on those cars, because the fix while it is a seal is trivial and the fix once it is a module is not.

What we inspect that the sheet does not list

The booklet was written for a new car. The cars we see are not new, and each engine family has a short list of things that happen to it that no service sheet mentions. We look for them at every A and B, and we photograph what we find.

  • M272 and M273 (2005–2012 V6 and V8): the engine-oil cooler seals in the valley between the heads, which weep oil down the back of the engine and smell of it after a drive; the intake-manifold swirl flaps; and on the early M272, the balance-shaft sprocket, which shows itself as camshaft-correlation codes before anything else. We covered the first of these in the case studies.
  • M276 and M278 (2011 on, direct injection): timing-chain and camshaft-adjuster noise on cold start, and the turbocharger oil-feed lines on the M278.
  • Every engine: the plastic thermostat housing and expansion tank, the electric auxiliary coolant pump on cars that have one, and the drive belt's tensioner, which fails before the belt does.
  • The camshaft-adjuster magnets: which can weep oil into their own connector and along the harness to the engine control module. A drop of oil in the connector is a cheap fix. A module full of it is not.
  • AIRMATIC cars: ride height at all four corners after the car has sat, and the compressor's run-time counter in XENTRY. The dedicated guide below explains why.
  • Cars with SBC brakes (the 2003–2006 E-Class and CLS, and the SL of that era): the pump's activation count, which XENTRY reports and which sets the unit's remaining life.
  • The battery and the auxiliary battery: tested under load; a weak one on a modern Mercedes-Benz produces warnings that have nothing to do with the battery.

Does an independent service keep the warranty?

Yes. Federal law prevents a manufacturer from denying warranty coverage because routine maintenance or repairs were done by an independent shop, provided the work meets the specification; the FTC's page in the sources says so in plain English. Our technicians are Mercedes-Benz factory-trained, the fluids meet the sheet, the reset is recorded in the car the same way the dealer records it, and we report the service to CARFAX. The fuller comparison is in dealership vs. independent shop.
